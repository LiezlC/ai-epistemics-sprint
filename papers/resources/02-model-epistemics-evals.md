# Track 1: Model Epistemics & Decision-Making Evals

Captured 9 November 2026 from the [Apart Research sprint resource page](https://apartresearch.com/sprints/ai-x-epistemics-research-sprint-2026-11-13-to-2026-11-15#resources). I checked each assigned item against the linked primary page, paper, repository, or legal text. Seven research papers have local PDFs and text extracts. ## Reading list

### Design Sketches: Collective Epistemics

[Owen Cotton-Barratt, Lizka Vaintrob, and Oly Sourbut, “Design Sketches: Collective Epistemics” (Forethought, 2025)](https://www.forethought.org/research/design-sketches-collective-epistemics). **Read:** full source article, including the epistemic virtue evals section. **Local:** [PDF](../design-sketches-collective-epistemics.pdf) · [text](../text/design-sketches-collective-epistemics.txt) · [Forethought download](https://www.forethought.org/api/article/design-sketches-collective-epistemics.pdf). This is a proposal, not an empirical result. It sketches a maintained benchmark spanning calibration, clarity, precision, bias, sycophancy, manipulation, and claim-level truth checks. The authors also flag benchmark gaming and recommend varying evaluation methods. **Project use:** treat this as the broad design brief, then narrow the sprint build to a falsifiable behavioral property. An Inspect task can report grader disagreement and sensitivity to paraphrase, pressure, and evidence provenance.

### Empirical papers

#### The MASK Benchmark

[Richard Ren et al., “The MASK Benchmark: Disentangling Honesty From Accuracy in AI Systems” (arXiv v3, 2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.03750). **Read:** current v3 paper, 22 pages. **Local:** [PDF](../mask-benchmark.pdf) · [text](../text/mask-benchmark.txt). MASK elicits a model’s neutral answer to a proposition, then applies a pressure prompt and checks whether the pressured statement conflicts with its elicited belief. The dataset contains 1,500 human-curated examples, including a 500-item held-out set; results cover 30 models. The authors report that factual accuracy rises with model scale while honesty does not, and that representation-engineering interventions can reduce lying rates. **Limits:** the operational measure is an inconsistency between elicited belief and pressured statement, with intent inferred from scenario design. It focuses on explicit lies of commission, not omission, and depends on reliable belief elicitation and automated mapping. **Project use:** add source-lineage controls to the pressure setup. Compare confidence when multiple agents repeat a claim from one upstream observation against the same number of agents reporting independent observations. Keep truth, agreement, and provenance as separate scores.

#### DarkBench

[Esben Kran et al., “DarkBench: Benchmarking Dark Patterns in Large Language Models” (ICLR 2025 oral)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.10728). **Read:** full 20-page paper. **Local:** [PDF](../darkbench.pdf) · [text](../text/darkbench.txt). The authors test 660 prompts in six categories: brand bias, user retention, sycophancy, anthropomorphism, harmful generation, and sneaking. They evaluate systems from five companies and report instances of developer-favoring behavior and untruthful communication. **Limits:** the categories are derived mainly from incentives in chatbot subscription businesses, so they do not cover every developer incentive or product context. Private system prompts also limit what researchers can observe about commercial systems. Prompt-based prevalence is not a measure of real-world deployment or user impact. **Project use:** borrow the adversarial prompt discipline, but test epistemic dependence directly. Give otherwise identical agents either copied source material or independent observations, then see whether the final model falsely treats repetitions as corroboration.

#### SycEval

[Aaron Fanous et al., “SycEval: Evaluating LLM Sycophancy” (AIES 2025)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.08177). **Read:** full 8-page paper, current arXiv v4. **Local:** [PDF](../syceval.pdf) · [text](../text/syceval.txt). The authors evaluate ChatGPT-4o, Claude Sonnet, and Gemini 1.5 Pro on math and medical question sets, using rebuttals to distinguish answer changes that correct an error from changes that make a correct answer wrong. They report sycophancy in 58.19% of cases, including 14.66% regressive changes that turn correct answers incorrect; the paper also measures persistence across turns. **Limits:** only three model versions and two structured domains are covered. Synthetic rebuttals may not represent the range of real user interactions, and some judgments use an LLM grader. The measured rates are benchmark-specific. **Project use:** preserve the progressive/regressive distinction. In a handoff eval, score warranted updating when new independent evidence arrives separately from capitulation to repeated claims that share a source.

#### ELEPHANT

[Myra Cheng et al., “ELEPHANT: Measuring and Understanding Social Sycophancy in LLMs” (arXiv v2, 2025)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.13995). **Read:** full 34-page paper. **Local:** [PDF](../elephant-social-sycophancy.pdf) · [text](../text/elephant-social-sycophancy.txt). ELEPHANT extends sycophancy measurement beyond explicit factual disagreement to face-saving and affirmation of a user’s desired self-image. Across 11 models, the authors report more face preservation than human responses in advice and wrongdoing scenarios; models affirm both sides of a moral conflict in 48% of paired cases. They also find social sycophancy rewarded in preference data. **Limits:** the benchmark is English-only and its face framework draws on Western, individualistic interaction theory. Some measurements rely on stochastic generations from proprietary models. **Project use:** build paired-perspective cases: give agents opposing positions while holding the evidence constant, then check whether the final answer tracks the evidence or mirrors the latest speaker. Include provenance so repeated copied testimony cannot masquerade as independent corroboration.

#### Why Language Models Hallucinate

[Adam Tauman Kalai, Ofir Nachum, Santosh S. Vempala, and Edwin Zhang, “Why Language Models Hallucinate” (OpenAI, 2025)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.04664). **Read:** full 36-page paper. **Local:** [PDF](../why-language-models-hallucinate.pdf) · [text](../text/why-language-models-hallucinate.txt). The paper models hallucination as a statistical classification error and argues that common binary grading rewards guessing over abstention. It recommends changing scoring in existing high-profile benchmarks so uncertainty and abstention receive appropriate credit. **Limits:** the formal analysis simplifies open-ended generation and ambiguity; it does not directly establish how often a given deployed model hallucinates in a user setting. Its proposed mitigation is an incentive argument rather than a controlled field trial. **Project use:** make calibrated uncertainty a primary outcome. Score the final agent on whether it distinguishes one observation repeated through several handoffs from independent evidence, and reward an explicit “source count is one” response when warranted.

#### ForecastBench

[Ezra Karger et al., “ForecastBench: A Dynamic Benchmark of AI Forecasting Capabilities” (ICLR 2025)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.19839). **Read:** current v5, full 37-page paper. **Local:** [PDF](../forecastbench.pdf) · [text](../text/forecastbench.txt). ForecastBench generates and refreshes 1,000 questions whose outcomes are unresolved when forecasters answer, reducing leakage from known resolutions. The paper compares human experts, the public, and language models on a random 200-question subset; expert forecasters beat the best tested LLM with p < .001. **Limits:** the comparison covers a subset of one evolving question pool and a particular model and prompt setup. Forecast quality depends on question selection, resolution criteria, and scoring choices. It does not itself test provenance between agent handoffs. **Project use:** use the live, resolvable-question idea if the sprint team wants a delayed validation phase. For a weekend study, keep the outcome set controlled and report question-level uncertainty rather than claiming broad forecasting ability.

### Tools and legal reference

#### Petri

[Anthropic, “Petri: An Open-Source AI Auditing Tool”](https://www.anthropic.com/research/petri-open-source-auditing). **Read:** primary project page and linked code. Petri is an agentic auditing framework that runs models through scenarios and records behavior for evaluator review. It is software documentation, not a paper or an independent benchmark result. **Project use:** inspect its task and transcript structure for multi-turn auditing patterns; retain a transparent, human-checkable rubric for whether agents identify duplicated sources.

#### Bloom

[Anthropic, “Introducing Bloom: Automated Behavioral Evals”](https://www.anthropic.com/research/bloom). **Read:** primary project page and linked implementation. Bloom automates parts of behavioral evaluation, including generation of evaluation scenarios and analysis of model behavior. It is a tool description, not evidence that a proposed rubric is valid. **Project use:** use it as a comparator for automated case generation; manually audit generated cases for source-dependence and scorer errors.

#### EU AI Act, Article 5

The sprint points to a third-party transcription of [Article 5](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/5/). For legal wording, prefer the [official consolidated text on EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/en/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A02024R1689-20260727), which includes Article 5 and subsequent amendments. The rule targets specified manipulative or deceptive techniques that materially distort behavior, impair informed decisions, and cause or risk significant harm, alongside other prohibited practices. It does not supply an agreed model-evaluation protocol or thresholds. **Project use:** any measurement proposal should map observable behavior to the elements of the legal test and avoid presenting a benchmark score as a legal finding.

## Example-project links and consistency check

I checked the Track 1 example links against the resource list and primary destinations. The overlaps resolve as stated: [MASK](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.03750), [DarkBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.10728), [ForecastBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.19839), [SycEval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.08177), [ELEPHANT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.13995), the [Collective Epistemics design sketch](https://www.forethought.org/research/design-sketches-collective-epistemics), and the [EU AI Act Article 5](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/5/) all match the titles and topics on their destination pages. The [Consistency Checks for Language Model Forecasters paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.18544) is an additional paper linked only from the Confidence Audit example. **Read:** current v2 full 55-page paper. **Local:** [PDF](../consistency-checks-language-model-forecasters.pdf) · [text](../text/consistency-checks-language-model-forecasters.txt). Its arbitrage-based consistency metric correlates with later Brier scores in the tested forecasters, and it releases a benchmark designed to resolve in 2028. This is a useful adjacent method, with a clear boundary: internal consistency can correlate with accuracy yet can be gamed without improved accuracy. The [Inspect repository](https://github.com/UKGovernmentBEIS/inspect_ai) is also correctly linked. The README identifies it as the UK AI Security Institute’s open evaluation framework, with support for multi-turn dialog, solvers, scorers, and a collection of prebuilt evals.

One precision point for the example descriptions: “Confidence Audit” refers to confidence in recommendations whose outcomes resolve later, whereas ForecastBench and Consistency Checks are forecasting benchmarks. ForecastBench measures resolved forecasting performance; Consistency Checks tests logical consistency as an earlier signal. These are related methods, not interchangeable validations of decision quality.

## Candidate sprint study

### Does repeated testimony inflate model confidence?

Use matched synthetic cases with a known answer and source graph. In one condition, two or three agent messages repeat an observation copied from one source. In the comparison condition, the same number of messages report genuinely independent observations. Keep wording, answer distribution, and agent roles balanced. Randomize whether provenance is shown to the final model.

Measure final-answer accuracy, stated confidence, abstention, source count, and whether the model calls repetition independent corroboration. Separate useful updating after new evidence from confidence inflation caused by duplicated evidence. Use blinded human labels for the source graph and adjudicate grader disagreements. This isolates a failure mode that sycophancy and answer-flip benchmarks approach but do not directly test: correlated testimony passed through handoffs can look like multiple confirmations. Related work includes [Bara et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.01873) and [Zhou et al. (EMNLP 2026)](https://aclanthology.org/2026.emnlp-main.509/); see [PROJECT-ANGLES](../../PROJECT-ANGLES.md) for the overlap analysis and the narrowed candidate claim.

### Inspect implementation sketch

Represent each case with a stable ID, gold answer, evidence items, provenance parent IDs, and a condition label. An Inspect task can run the same case through scripted agent handoffs, then ask a final solver for an answer, confidence, and an evidence account. Use deterministic checks for provenance recognition and answer accuracy; use a separately reviewed rubric for confidence calibration and warranted updating. Save full transcripts, model/version and sampling settings, prompt variants, and scorer outputs. Report case-level results and uncertainty, plus examples of grader disagreement. This yields a small auditable benchmark before attempting broad model comparisons.
